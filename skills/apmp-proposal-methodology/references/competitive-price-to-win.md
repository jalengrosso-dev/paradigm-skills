# Competitive Price To Win (cPTW)

cPTW assesses how competitors will offer solutions to meet the customer's requirements, using
customer and competitor intelligence to find both what the customer values and what competitors are
likely to offer. Its output is never just a number — it shows the **capability-vs-cost tradeoff each
competitor will present as evaluated by the buying customer**, so your team can build a solution that
raises win probability and meets business goals.

## What cPTW is (and is not)

- cPTW is **externally focused** and **independent** of your solutioning/costing/pricing/proposal
  work. It assesses the whole **"Big S" solution** — technical, management, past performance, and
  cost/price — competitors will offer based on *their* understanding of the customer and opportunity.
- cPTW is **not pricing.** cPTW sets the **target position** you must reach to beat competitors;
  **pricing** is the internal process you use to convert your own cost to a bid price that tries to
  hit (or, per your business goals, deliberately miss) that target.
- Because it's independent, it is free of the "we're the best" bias that every capture effort
  carries. Some firms call it "deal to win" or "position to win" to stress it's more than price.

## Key definitions

- **Cost** — resources to supply a solution: direct (labor, materials, travel) + indirect (overhead,
  burdens).
- **Price** — what you charge the customer in the bid. Generally higher cost → higher price, but in a
  hypercompetitive market price is strategy, not just "cost plus profit."
- **Costing & pricing** — internal processes: develop your own cost, then convert to a bid price per
  your strategy.
- **Business intelligence** — internal analysis of your own company's data/performance (distinct from
  competitive intelligence).
- **Addressable funding** — maximum funding available to the customer minus the customer's own costs;
  the ceiling available to the awardee.

## The two analyses

1. **Price-to-Compete (top-down).** *Before* detailed requirements are known, use historical award
   and funding data to see where competitors tend to bid and where the customer tends to award
   relative to budget. Feeds the pursue/no-pursue decision and an early solution shape.
2. **Price-to-Win (bottom-up).** *Once* requirements, acquisition approach, and evaluation processes
   are known, analyze the tradeoffs among the customer's requirements/budget/values and each
   competitor's likely objectives, capabilities, solution, costs, and strategies — to fine-tune your
   solution or justify walking away.

Both are fed by **competitive analysis** (the intelligence effort). Early **strategic** competitor
analysis (broad capabilities/capacities/strategies in a market) helps find market gaps; opportunity-
specific **tactical** competitive analysis studies the specific solutions competitors will offer,
including who defines requirements and makes the award.

## Roles

- **CA analyst** — ethically gathers and analyzes competitive-intelligence data to produce
  competitive-analysis results.
- **PTW / cPTW analyst** — uses that intelligence to produce Price-to-Win results.
- On smaller opportunities one person/team may do both. Either may be internal staff or external
  consultants — but must remain **independent** of the capture/proposal team to avoid bias.

## Critical success factors

- A high-level executive **champion** to overcome objections.
- Realistic expectations from the start.
- Decision-makers' information needs understood and met.
- Independence from the capture effort.
- Truth valued even when uncomfortable.
- Adequate, appropriate resources.
- Information-sharing valued at all levels.
- **Rely on intelligence, not intuition:** ethically gathered data, clearly stated assumptions,
  accurate models → defensible results, even when leadership hoped for a different number.

## Top-down (price-to-compete) method

1. Gather and analyze **funding data** from past acquisitions by the same customer (sources and uses).
2. Identify, for each past acquisition, **who won**, the **total contract value**, and the **total
   evaluated price** (usually different numbers).
3. For each targeted competitor, pull their past awards (customer/opportunity, contract value,
   evaluated price).
4. Project award tendencies of both the customer and competitors. **Never present point values** —
   show **comfort-zone graphics** so leadership can decide pursue/no-pursue and shape an early
   solution.

## Bottom-up (price-to-win) method

1. Identify each competitor's **cost elements** and the strategies/tactics they'll use to turn cost
   into bid price.
2. Use a templated **notional solution** on a clearly defined **work breakdown structure** so all
   required cost elements are captured and every competitor model is consistent.
3. Evaluate each competitor's solution through the **customer's evaluation criteria** to show how the
   non-cost factors (technical, management, past performance) will be viewed.
4. Combine non-cost evaluation with the numeric PTW models to reveal the **optimum Price-to-Win
   position** — the target with the highest win probability.

## Refinement, changes, and lessons learned

- Begin as early as possible and **iterate to award.** Update models when the solicitation changes —
  ask **"who benefits?"** from each change to gauge competitive impact. Watch for competitors
  dropping out (they may free up subs/suppliers others can absorb) and re-run intelligence.
- Build **"what-if" planning** for likely scenarios; you can't foresee all, but plan the likely ones
  and stay flexible.
- Recommend **strategies and tactics** to hit the PTW position (highlight your strengths, counter
  competitor strengths, exploit weaknesses via SWOT/Five Forces) — but the decision to follow them is
  leadership's.
- **Post-award**, compare your PTW result to the winning price and identify what differentiated the
  winner; feed lessons learned back into the knowledge base and future bids. Lessons aren't useful
  unless applied.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating PTW as **just a number** — it's a *position* between evaluated price and evaluated
  capability, seen through the customer's eyes.
- Believing PTW reviews **your** solution — it never includes your capture/proposal team's solution,
  cost, or pricing ability; it's externally derived from customer and competitor behavior.
- Thinking PTW is "the lowest price from our own bottom-up costing" — it's the target of price *and*
  capability you must hit to win.
- Believing PTW works **without** competitive analysis — the "c" in cPTW is required; without clear
  data, results aren't defensible or actionable.
- Treating it as **"one and done"** — continue customer/competitor analysis to award and provide
  updated results iteratively.
- Forgetting it's **not an exact science** — winners use cPTW as one data point in solutioning;
  losers blame "bad" cPTW instead of identifying every factor in the loss.
