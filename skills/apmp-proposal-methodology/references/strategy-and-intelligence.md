## Customer relationship management (know / like / trust)

Across sectors, customers work with those they **know, like, and trust** over the competition. They
still must follow regulations and score you against criteria — but if they trust you, they find a
way to pick you; if they don't, they find a way not to. CRM is the *managed* process (not ad hoc)
of getting inside the customer's head and earning that preference before the RFP.

**Start early.** It is permissible and expected to interact with customers before and during the
process — listen to needs, show how you solve them. Before release you can attempt **RFP shaping**:
influencing the requirement so it reflects your discriminators. Shaping gets harder as release
nears; many customers drop a "cone of silence" at or before a draft RFP.

**Contact plan** — who you'll contact and who attends; what you want to learn and what message you
want to land; when (sometimes parallel meetings at different levels to compare); where; why (what
you hope to accomplish); how you'll communicate the message.

## Opportunity/capture strategy vs. tactics

- **Strategy** = your pre-engagement position (what you'll be).
- **Tactics** = the actions that implement and convey it persuasively.

Align the whole hierarchy so one message runs through everything: **business strategy** (overall
objectives) → **market strategy** (a market, many sales) → **opportunity/capture strategy** (this
opportunity; identical to *sales* strategy and to *win* strategy) → **proposal strategy** (a subset
that writes the persuasive proposal — same message, different tactical implementation).

Analyze your **current position** (you always have one, whether you know it or not), then work to
improve it versus competitors. Use standard tools/templates so the strategy and its implementation
are consistent across the team. Write **strategy statements** that pair the strategic "what" (your
position) with the tactical "how" (the actions), and note that one statement may be implemented
across several action plans.

## Common definitions — hold the team to these

- **Issues** — customer concerns/worry items (what keeps them up at night).
- **Motivators** — objectives they want to achieve: improved profit, increased sales, reduced cost,
  improved safety, reduced risk, improved quality.
- **Hot buttons** — the consolidated ≤5 highest-priority issues+motivators for a specific
  opportunity. Record during capture in the customer's exact words; align the solution to them; cite
  them in the executive summary and weave matching themes throughout.

## Buyers — identify all three and list each one's issues

- **Economic buyers** — give final purchase approval; weigh price vs. performance; focus on
  bottom-line impact.
- **Users** — judge impact on their own job performance; concerns are often emotional/subjective:
  reliability, support, ease of operation, maintenance, safety, morale, personal success.
- **Technical buyers** — gatekeepers; can't give a final yes but can give a final no; often set the
  shortlist; score features against objective specs.

## Bidder Comparison Matrix (BCM) and SWOT

The BCM (a SWOT variant) focuses on **customer issues** (stated and unstated) and compares the
customer's *current perception* of your solution and organization against their perception of each
competitor. Use it repeatedly to measure whether your positioning actions are working.

- Score from the **customer's perspective**, not yours — be unbiased or you'll inflate your own
  scores and miss weaknesses. If time is short, use High/Medium/Low ratings; some teams weight
  issues to 100 and rate self and competition on each.
- Build a **SWOT** from the customer's perception of each bidder; derive strategies at quadrant
  intersections (e.g., Strength × Opportunity = "Maxi-Maxi"). Competitor weaknesses = your
  opportunities; their strengths = your threats. The completed SWOT drives the win strategy.
- **Hygiene factors:** where every bidder is expected to reach the same standard (or no one has a
  track record), mark the field "all equal" — the best move is to remind the customer all bidders
  are equal there, and spend your energy on real discriminators.

## Value propositions in the strategy phase

Draft value propositions early, one per buyer type, and develop them *with* the customer where you
can — customer collaboration increases acceptance of the numbers. (Full treatment in
`persuasion-toolkit.md`.)

## Tradeoffs and ghosting

- **Tradeoffs** show you considered alternatives and picked the best value at acceptable risk for
  the customer — not just your usual approach.
- **Ghosting** = raising the weakness of an alternative you rejected, when a competitor offers that
  alternative. Don't name competitors (ethics); independent, third-party evidence lets you press
  harder. The best-laid capture plans must transition cleanly into the proposal, or the customer —
  who's been talking with you — will be confused by a proposal that doesn't match expectations.

## Customer & competitor intelligence

- **Customer intelligence** — the customer's spoken and unspoken needs and the qualities they seek
  in a contractor; built over months/years, mostly from direct customer contact. A good working
  relationship between the capture and proposal teams is what gets this into the narrative.
- **Competitive intelligence** — objectively understanding competitors' strengths, weaknesses, and
  strategies as *perceived by the target customer*. It is an **ethical** business practice (guided by
  SCIP and corporate legal/ethics rules): use only open sources, never deception, phishing,
  impersonation, or proprietary data without permission. Most of what you need is available from
  colleagues and public sources; verify via group brainstorming.
- Build a **collection plan** aimed at the decisions leaders must make (gate reviews, solutioning,
  proposal). Start early, continue throughout, and foster information sharing across the extended
  organization (a shared, accessible knowledge base helps).
- Distinguish **primary sources** (original data, e.g., budget documents) from **secondary sources**
  (analyses of primary data, e.g., news). Adopt the sources your customer itself uses.

## Strategy and win themes over time

Strategy and themes evolve as intelligence matures; each time the customer/competitor/capability
assessments update, reassess strategies and win themes.

- Pick a **single unifying concept** (a word or two) that summarizes the strategy; make sure everyone
  understands it; all win themes link back to it. Expand it into an **elevator speech** that gains
  quantified benefits as intelligence improves.
- Use feedback from capture-plan and competitor reviews to sharpen themes and surface new
  discriminators. Allocate each piece of evidence to where it belongs in the proposal as soon as the
  outline exists. Design a customer-focused **cover and key graphic** to sharpen the message and
  kick-start the executive-summary mock-up.
- Engage teaming partners **early** — late partnering skips proposal-strategy development and yields
  generic, unsubstantiated partner content.

**Litmus test:** if a competitor saw your proposal strategy, it would damage your chances of winning.
If revealing it wouldn't hurt you, it isn't a strategy yet.
