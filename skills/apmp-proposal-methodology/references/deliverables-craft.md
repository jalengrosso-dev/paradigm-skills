# Deliverable Craft

How to build the artifacts an evaluator actually scores. Evaluators **scan** rather than read, so
every rule here serves one goal: make the right answer fast to find and easy to award points to.

## Compliance matrix

A checklist for you and the evaluator that maps every RFP requirement to where it's answered. Use one
on **every** bid even when not required, and place a version before the first section that references
requirements. If the customer provides their own matrix, treat its items as "must-haves."

- **Build it early** — in tandem with the outline, before content planning and writing. Building it
  at the end is "drawing blueprints after the house is built."
- **Parallel the RFP structure** exactly (numbering, naming); begin each item with the RFP's action
  verb (identify, discuss…) to signal to writers what's expected.
- **Shred line by line** — one row per shall/will/must; build in a spreadsheet so it re-sorts by RFP
  or proposal section after amendments/outline changes.
- **Keep it current** — update after any change to the outline, the team (authors), or the RFP; assign
  one owner to maintain it.
- **Cover three compliance dimensions:** submittal instructions (format, packaging, forms, right
  place/person/time), requirements (can you deliver? is the team available? underlying needs solved?),
  and internal compliance (legal, CRM entry, approvals, authorized to submit).
- **Flag disqualifiers** — a non-compliant response there means the proposal isn't evaluated; fix any
  partial/non-comply toward compliance where possible.
- **Use it to sell** — add a brief statement that proves compliance *and* restates a win theme or
  discriminator, so an evaluator reading only the matrix understands the solution.
- **Watch for hidden requirements** buried in narrative; don't assume the customer's matrix and the
  RFP have the same count.

## Proposal organization (and BLUF)

Well-organized proposals give a roadmap, make key points easy to find, are written from the
customer's point of view, and use highlighting (headings, roadmaps, graphics) so evaluators can scan.

- **Plan content before writing** (writing comes last). Use the customer's structure and numbering,
  even if it fights your preferred narrative; if you deviate, explain why in the text.
- **Layer information:** introduce the topic at a high level → add supporting facts/claims (features
  and benefits) → give proof (examples, case studies) → address drawbacks or ghost the competition →
  conclude and reinforce.
- **Put the most important points first** — in the proposal, each section, each paragraph, each list.
- **BLUF** (bottom line up front): state the most important information first, then context, then
  reinforcing detail. Use it for the whole proposal or per section.
- Watch for the same question **scattered across volumes** — coordinate so multiple authors don't
  answer it differently.

## Headings

Headings act like headlines; they help evaluators grasp organization and key points and can raise
scores.

- Follow the RFP's exact heading content, numbering, and levels when specified. When not specified,
  reuse the bid request's main/sub headings so evaluators find answers fast (don't copy misspellings).
- Prefer **active headings** that carry a benefit/discriminator ("Our E-Gates v0.6 reduced check-in
  to 7 minutes") over telegraphic labels ("About us"). Reserve **telegraphic** headings for standard
  sections (Executive Summary, Appendices).
- **Verb headings** convey action (keep tense consistent); **noun headings** give brief direct answers
  to bid questions; avoid question headings (they signal uncertainty).
- Create a clear hierarchy; keep to **≤3 numbered levels**, then use **run-in headings**. Keep
  headings **parallel** (like items phrased alike) and consistent in style; vary appearance
  deliberately in this order: size, boldface, color, italics, font change, all caps, underline.
- Use an **informal table of contents / section introduction** — a short intro paragraph that
  previews the section and can pre-sell before the evaluator reaches the detail; keep subheadings
  identical to the topics listed there.

## Graphics

Clear, audience-focused graphics (including covers) improve win rates, comprehension, recall, and
emotional resonance. A 3M/University of Minnesota study found visual-aided presentations ~43% more
persuasive; graphics + words beat either alone.

- **Reflect the customer's motivations.** Graphics fail when made "by the author, for the author."
  Understand the evaluator's needs/goals/pain points; adjust content and even aesthetics (a cost-
  pressured customer may distrust an expensive-looking proposal).
- **Process:** write the one-sentence **summary / action caption first**, then **Summarize → Explain
  → Visualize → Render**. Create key graphics **early** (before writing) and use the executive-summary
  graphic as a roadmap; conceptualized graphics double as storyboards.
- **Validate objectively** — Goal (does it answer a crucial question or emphasize why to choose you?),
  Content (understandable without the caption? all content relevant to the caption?), Aesthetics
  (every choice purposeful and consistent?). Have someone outside the team check comprehension.
- **Design principles:** color (intentional, culturally aware), style, consistency, grid, balance,
  shapes/lines (diagonals = dynamic; horizontal/vertical = stable; curves = calming).
- **Formats:** vector vs. raster by use; build graphics at final display size to avoid font/size
  issues. Use **templates** and a defined visual vocabulary. Manage assets centrally with naming
  conventions, version control, metadata, backups, and style guides.
- **Copyright:** don't use images you don't have rights to (images are copyrighted by default);
  confirm commercial (not editorial-only) licensing. Avoid visual noise; design for accessibility.

## Page & document design

The document's look is nearly as important as its content; it makes an impression and must be
compliant and consistent.

- **Lock a document template early** and give it to the whole team: final production method
  (print/electronic), margins, columns, header/footer style, font family, treatment of body text /
  headings / bullets / emphasis / callouts, colors, and graphics handling. Never stray from customer-
  specified paper size, margins, page limits, or fonts; absent specs, follow corporate branding, then
  industry norm.
- Fonts: choose common, legible faces; **no smaller than 10–12pt** (8pt only inside graphics/tables).
- **Deliver as PDF** to lock layout/fonts, control file size, and (advanced) add security, forms,
  hyperlinks. Maintain version control rigorously.
- Six layout strategies: **contrast, proximity, white space, consistency, balance, alignment.**
  Balance/asymmetry can signal brand personality (innovative vs. established).

## Cost & pricing data

Cost and pricing depend heavily on setting — the biggest government-vs-commercial divergence in the
lifecycle. **Government/pseudo-government** buyers often require **certified cost-or-pricing data**
reasonably available at agreement, and flow-down of that requirement to subcontractors — flag this to
the Compliance and Price-to-Win agents; understand acquisition rules before bidding.

- Show what comprises your price to demonstrate a logical approach and build credibility — in numbers
  *and* graphics.
- Distinguish **price / value / added value.** Winning prices usually satisfy: *customer's perceived
  value ≥ customer's budget ≥ your price ≥ your cost.* Added value = perceived value − (price +
  implementation cost); customers favor the greatest added value. Quantify it (show how much more
  effective your solution is, in real money) — an unquantified "we add value" claim is empty.
- Include a **cost executive summary** that ties to the technical proposal and carries the message you
  want the price analyst to deliver to the decision-maker; give traceable detail plus summaries.
- Use **graphics** (pie/bar/line, feature-benefit tables) to convey value fast. Substantiate all cost
  claims with facts, not judgment.

## Managing questions to the customer (Q&A)

After release, manage the Q&A period swiftly and strategically; focus questions primarily on being
able to submit a fully **compliant** proposal.

- Assign one **primary + secondary POC** so the customer gets consistent, not conflicting, messages.
- Track deadlines; **submit early** — customers batch answers, and an early question yields an earlier
  answer you can act on. Engage the whole org (technical, contracts, finance, legal) to generate and
  review questions.
- For each question, give the **exact section identifier and wording** needing clarification (vague
  questions force the customer to hunt and get vaguer answers).
- Use **customer terminology or generic language** to stay customer-focused and protect anonymity/
  strategy — assume every communication is shared with all vendors. Keep questions **short and single-
  pointed**; multi-part questions read as confusion or an attempt to change requirements. If you have
  no questions, say so (fewer questions = less work for the customer).
- **Track all Q&A** — yours and others' — as competitor intelligence. Government processes are more
  formal; commercial less so, and questions may or may not be shared. Never use a question to
  challenge what the customer wishes to procure.

## Relevant past performance

Past-performance descriptions show you have relevant management and technical experience and a track
record — evidence you can meet the requirement. Distinguish **proposal risk** (the future risk of your
proposed approach, assessed by technical evaluators) from **performance risk** (the probability you'll
perform, based on your record, assessed by contracts professionals).

- **Review the whole RFP for all PP-related requirements** — not just the PP section but the SOW/PWS.
  Determine how many descriptions are required and whether teammates'/subs' PP is needed. Assess the
  workload against the master schedule and pull in extra writers early.
- **Choose candidates by relevance, recency, quality of reference.** Relevance components include
  **scope** (how closely the candidate matches the technical work — many large bidders count
  themselves compliant if every SOW/PWS section is covered by at least one submitted contract).
- Mirror **SOW/PWS keywords** — evaluations are often keyword-searched. Design descriptions to be
  **skimmed** (expect the evaluator to read only the first page or search for keywords and verify the
  reference). Support PP claims throughout the proposal with proof points, using signal phrases ("This
  approach succeeded when…"). Allow enough time for development and approval.

## Resumes

Positioning your people is often central to the offer. Show the customer your personnel are **actual,
fully available at contract start, and more qualified than any competitor's** — promises to hire after
award are insufficient; always name individuals.

- **Team selection** — match the customer's sector/vision/values/hot buttons and requirements to your
  people's hard and soft skills.
- **Gather/screen materials** — corporate resume, "street resume," any reuse resumes, photo/signature
  if needed, solution-specific stories. Verify each person meets RFP requirements (clearance,
  education, certs, years, specific experience). If someone doesn't fit, replace them now, not during
  writing.
- **Standard compliant template**, easy to skim: position/labor category (with RFP section headings) →
  key qualifications up top → similar-customer work → education/training/certifications → testimonials/
  awards. Put education after relevant experience.
- A **writer drafts** the resume (not the SME) for efficiency/consistency; ensure every bullet has a
  customer-focused, measurable benefit; write clearly and short even when the work is complex.
- Maintain a **corporate resume database** (update as part of performance reviews); add proposal-
  specific resumes to it or to a reuse database. **Tailor** every resume to the RFP — include only the
  experience and qualifications the RFP calls for; emphasize accomplishments over responsibilities.

## Presentations

Any capture/proposal team member may present internally or to the customer. Make every aspect
audience-focused; most presenters wrongly focus on themselves and use slides as speaker notes.

- Start from **what you want to happen after** the presentation; think about the audience, not what's
  easiest to talk about; create content first, then move relevant elements to slides.
- **Structure around three** (the brain grasps three points easily): why it matters + key elements →
  each element in priority order with support → summary. Use **BLUF** where apt.
- Slides are **visual media** — keywords and visuals, not full sentences; if the audience could read
  your talk off the slides, there's too much text. Avoid bullet-point walls; animate points to appear
  one at a time (no distracting fly-ins). Make **benefit-led slide titles** ("The three areas where we
  bring the most value," not "Summary"). Delegate slide design if you lack the skill — audiences
  equate slide quality with your quality. Review, rehearse, and match method to facilities/occasion/
  audience/your experience.
