# Tools & Systems

The reusable machinery that makes the process repeatable: the plans, schedules, outlines, knowledge
base, and standards that let a team produce compliant, responsive, on-time proposals without
reinventing the wheel each time.

## Proposal Management Plans (PMP)

A high-quality bid is always based on careful planning before any writing. The PMP allocates effort,
structures work, and enforces timelines. Plans are increasingly tailored by sector, bid size, and
type. Bids are complex projects with a must-meet end point (the deadline); like any project they
benefit from organization, and later tasks depend on earlier ones.

- **Prepare the PMP before kickoff**, evolving it from the opportunity/capture plan — often more than
  half the capture plan carries into the PMP, adding writers' packages, a preparation schedule, and an
  outline. Roll it out at kickoff for buy-in and a reality check.
- **Use templates** for smaller/routine bids, but fine-tune them — process overkill hurts proposals;
  keep documents user-friendly.
- **Ensure executive buy-in:** explain how plans benefit stakeholders, secure senior respect for the
  plan, name an owner/updater, define sign-off and change-sharing, and have leads use plans visibly.
- **Plan and organize content** at the right level: **overview plans** (few key stages), **action-
  based plans** (detailed, diagram-based, who does what when), and **content plans** (steps from idea
  to text).
- **Pitfalls:** allocating too little time to plan development; letting the plan become an end in
  itself — follow it closely but keep common sense and allow modifications.

## Scheduling

Scheduling visualizes, implements, and monitors the plan. Match complexity to the opportunity's
value/importance, timeframe, and resources.

- **Opportunity/capture schedules** are harder than proposal schedules — capture is limited by
  customer procurement steps and shifting end points; the earlier you find an opportunity, the less
  is known. Schedule intelligence-gathering and solution-positioning **as early as possible** (access
  is restricted after release) and continue until restricted; take a disciplined but adaptive
  approach.
- **Proposal schedules have a fixed end date** — build them **backward from the due date/time**.
  Estimate total effort, schedule delivery and production first, then earlier tasks; list tasks/
  subtasks with dependencies; set start/end dates using metrics from past bids. Sample schedules
  exist for 10-, 30-, and 60-day proposals.
- **Reserve time:** planning + infrastructure setup typically needs **10–20%** of total time; keep an
  additional **~10% in reserve** for the unforeseen. **Minimize sequential, maximize parallel** tasks;
  think through dependencies carefully.
- **Communicate clearly:** explain each task's start/end and expectations; put the schedule on a
  collaborative platform; break large assignments into short pieces with interim deadlines and
  immediate feedback. Let the schedule drive daily priorities.
- **Protect reviews and production:** allow enough time for review cycles (½ day to a week each) and
  be conservative with production (plan for worst cases — viruses, outages, equipment failures); set a
  firm change cutoff. **Change the schedule only for customer-driven events** (deadline extension or
  significant RFP change); frequent interim-deadline changes make people discount them.

## Opportunity/Capture plan development

Capture planning identifies opportunities, assesses the environment, and develops/manages winning
strategies to capture a specific opportunity. It requires written, action-oriented plans and is
initiated after the pursuit-decision milestone, continuing into program start-up. The aim: position
the customer to prefer your organization and solution before proposals are submitted. Primary
audience: those who manage, approve, or execute the plan, proposal, and resulting contract.

- A good capture plan is **realistic and specific** — objective, action, responsible individual,
  timing, review frequency. Benefits: more realistic understanding, better bid decisions, consensus/
  information transfer, saved cost, higher win rates.
- **Work the plan, don't just fill it out.** Move from unknown to known by researching, analyzing, and
  **validating data with the customer**; learn what competitors will offer, at what price, and what
  the customer thinks of them. Build a BCM, document each bidder's strengths/weaknesses, define your
  solution/strategy/cost/price against an independently developed **price-to-win** target, then
  implement action plans to influence the customer — iterating to a favored position.
- Keep the process **dynamic, flexible, current, collaborative**; present a **notional solution** to
  the customer for feedback and to shape requirements in your favor. Balance planning with **action**
  — detailed plans without action waste time.
- Use **consistent customer/competitor/capability analysis tools** throughout, even when time is
  short. Gain and keep **senior-management approval**; avoid the bias toward "comfortable information"
  — capture managers must be able to surface negative findings, and leadership must never punish
  honesty that points to a no-bid.
- **Commit your best people** (organizations that assign only who they can spare usually lose). Keep
  the Opportunity/Capture, Program, and Proposal Manager relationships balanced. **Initiate teaming
  and make/buy decisions early** (confidentiality agreement/MOU in place first) and involve teammates
  in strategy, competitor reviews, and kickoff.
- Assign **specific, measurable objectives with dates** to actions and individuals; most capture work
  is part-time, so managers must ensure completion — recommending a no-bid for lack of resources often
  focuses senior stakeholders on how important the opportunity really is. **The single biggest reason
  for losing competitive business is failing to adequately influence the customer before submittal.**
- Run **regular gate reviews** (Is it winnable? Do returns justify the spend? Is the team ready for
  the next phase? What more is needed to win?), based on the current plan; keep reviews short and
  action-focused. An **early draft executive summary** is a key capture-plan element and the bridge to
  proposal planning and the kickoff brief. Keep completed capture plans for reuse.
- **Pitfalls:** focusing on the plan rather than the customer; thinking one reviewed plan is enough —
  if no new information has been added since the last gate, question whether progress (or a no-bid) is
  due.

## Content plans (outlines, MECE, storyboards, mock-ups)

Winning content is **planned before it's created** — don't start writing until there's a clear, shared
view of the finished product. Correcting poor quality late means rework, risking a late or over-budget
proposal.

- **Systematic, requirements-driven** content design, documented and supported by tools/templates and
  training; flexible enough for different content and evaluation types.
- **Adjust planning depth** to time, opportunity importance, and team experience/size — the investment
  should return more than it costs. Experienced teams on familiar products need less; new customers/
  products or complex solutions and must-win bids justify detailed planning.
- **Build a requirements baseline** (requirements analysis matrix / checklist); for formal requests,
  strip stated/implied requirements, have SMEs analyze technical requirements and formulate customer
  questions, and follow the request's sequence/numbering. For informal requests, list known
  requirements from meetings/comms/account plan, group by category/priority, flag conflicts,
  consolidate.
- **Create a robust topical outline** (indented list, hierarchy, or mind map). Follow the customer's
  structure/numbering even if illogical (explain any deviation); where none is imposed, follow the
  bid-request order; add detail where evaluation weightings are highest; respect page/word limits,
  spending space where marks are greatest. Make the outline **MECE** (mutually exclusive, collectively
  exhaustive) and **uniquely assignable** (one accountable author per item).
- **Annotate the outline** for authors: graphics/action captions, boilerplate, RFP requirements to
  address, page/word limits, themes/strategies, customer terminology, the **question each section must
  answer**, and supporting proof points.
- **Detail for important sections** via **section content plans/storyboards** (sequential; build on
  what came before) or **mock-ups** (page-by-page visualization; interactive/top-down; aid printing).
  Apply the **"just enough" planning** principle — too much constrains the SME, too little invites
  non-compliance.
- **Involve writers** in content-plan creation; the Bid/Proposal Manager signs off before writing (the
  content plan is a contract between manager and writer). **Review content plans before writing**
  (horizontal: flow/consistency across sections; vertical: deep-dive into one). **Integrate into the
  proposal development plan** via a responsibility matrix (one accountable developer per item), page/
  graphic budgets, and reuse estimates from prior metrics.
- **Pitfalls:** too little planning (top-level outline + "add anything else you find" → orphan
  requirements and duplications); introducing techniques without training/support.

## Knowledge management

Knowledge = "understanding gained from experience." KM = getting the best knowledge to the right
person at the right time — capturing, standardizing, cataloging, and reapplying proposal content,
lessons learned, best practices, customer knowledge, and deal-crafting expertise. KM converts **tacit
→ explicit** and turns disorganized information into reusable objects under a useful taxonomy. KM is
more **change management than IT**.

- **Gain top-management sponsorship** (KM requires culture change) and **start small** with quick,
  low-cost initiatives to build momentum and prove value.
- **Assess status** via external **benchmarking** and internal **knowledge audits** (what knowledge
  exists, is needed, is missing, who needs it, how it's applied).
- **Communities of Practice** — from an informal in-company group to a professional body like APMP.
- **Create a sharing culture** (leadership, incentives) to overcome lack of awareness/capacity/
  relationships/motivation.
- **Standards for repurposing:** store knowledge as **discrete objects**, follow clear-writing
  principles, design for future use, build a taxonomy to label/associate related objects.

## Interviewing SMEs

Careful SME interviews surface tacit knowledge and success stories that bring a proposal to life and
demonstrate understanding of the customer's issues — often the real differentiator when products are
similar. Not all SMEs can turn knowledge into compelling content; a proposal writer who interviews
well performs a valuable service.

- **Decide what to learn** before writing questions; share topics with the SME to help them prepare;
  interview more than one SME for wide/controversial topics, but avoid contradictory viewpoints that
  leave little usable material.
- **Draft questions** with the specific SME in mind: understand the subject and SME; ask **open-ended**
  questions (not yes/no); apply **"So what?"** ("What advantage does that offer?"); end with a good
  **wrap-up** ("Is there something you wish I'd asked?").
- **Conduct** respectfully: set expectations, record, let it unfold naturally, ask follow-ups, take
  notes on key points.
- **Analyze**: summarize each answer in a sentence or two, structure into content sections, don't
  oversimplify away the complexity.
- **Validate**: send the SME the drafted content to confirm you captured their thinking and to expand.
- **Pitfall:** asking SMEs to write their own content — many can't write compellingly; assign writers
  early to support them.

## Library & writing standards

Consistency of information and style builds trust; a reusable library plus a style guide makes bidding
efficient and consistent.

- **Proposal library** — well-written, up-to-date reusable content pulled to answer common questions.
  Keep it current and relevant (the common failure). Key attributes: top-level sponsorship so SMEs own
  content; content management treated as a critical building block led by a trained specialist; good
  planning and metrics; content to the highest standard; **peer review before content enters the
  library** (as robust as a must-win review); a dedicated owner; constant updates as the story evolves.
- **Include** answers to frequent RFP questions, reusable graphics, past-performance write-ups, CVs,
  general company/governance/PM material (features/benefits/discriminators), and example/template plans
  (delivery, proposal, capture).
- **Build** from the best versions of your most-used, winning responses; edit for easy identification
  and reuse; store securely with role-based access (proposal software, SharePoint, or a shared drive).
- **Maintain** proposal-ready content formatted to corporate branding/style; **always tailor** reused
  content to the customer — generic, untailored content reads as lazy. Adapt basic details, mirror
  customer language/hot-button terminology, and adjust features/benefits/discriminators.
- **Writing style guide** — align to the brand and to APMP writing best practice (clear, direct,
  engaging; avoid jargon; accessible, informative). Set rules for formality/contractions, punctuation/
  grammar, spelling, and commonly confused words. The style guide is the starting point for **bid-
  specific** guides — adapt tone to each customer (a conversational tone may not always fit).
- **Pitfalls:** not creating a library/style guide; building the library from verbatim bid copies
  (loses the time savings, raises error risk); letting library/guide go stale (writers lose confidence
  and revert to copying recent proposals).
