# Process & Management

The end-to-end business-development machine and how to run it day to day: phases, gates, kickoff,
daily management, reviews, production, internal risk, and lessons learned. Organizations that follow
a defined BD process win more and spend less doing it.

## End-to-end process

An end-to-end BD process is a roadmap: it tells teams where they are, where they're going, and the
path to get there. Re-engineering one is usually triggered by restructuring, management change,
missed win-rate goals, staffing changes, sale of the org, maturing markets, or lost key
customers/contracts. **There is no one-size-fits-all model** — every organization designs its own,
tailored to its customers and gaining senior-executive buy-in; proven principles can be adapted.

Common features of most end-to-end processes:
- **BD phases** aligned to the customer's buying cycle (the generalized model has **8 phases**;
  see the Business Development Lifecycle topic for the phase definitions).
- **Structured bid decisions** giving executives visibility and control over what's pursued.
- **Team reviews** giving objective, constructive recommendations.
- **Defined outputs** that support a winning offer and become inputs to the next phase.

**Phases** are separated by **bid/gate decisions** (executive-led, business/strategic) that allocate
or withdraw BD resources. **Team reviews** improve the quality of thinking/documentation (tactical,
execution). **Outputs** feed the next phase — eliminate any output that doesn't advance the effort.
Plan more detail in advance when confidence about the requirement is higher (e.g., a draft RFP or a
recompete lets you form a conceptual solution and plan most sections).

## Gate (bid) decisions

A disciplined gate process makes effective compete/don't-compete decisions and raises win
probability. Bid decisions eliminate low-win-probability leads so resources concentrate on winnable
ones — **the most successful organizations bid less and win more.** A losing bid is the costliest
bid; a firm no-bid frees resources.

Every gate answers three questions: **Is it real? Can we win? Do we want to?** Gates are on a known,
adhered-to calendar, run by a tailored team (bid board / business acquisition panel, or a few named
individuals in smaller settings). Qualify opportunities by analyzing customer needs, your fit, and
expected competition.

Gate milestones (in order):
- **Market entry** (part of market identification/segmentation — different process; not opportunity-
  specific).
- **Opportunity qualification** — confirm the opportunity merits research/assessment resources
  (sometimes made at sales-management level).
- **Pursuit** — commit resources to capture planning and to influencing the customer to prefer you.
- **Bid/no-bid.**
- **Bid validation.**
- **Final review.**

Make a firm decision at each gate — proceed or stop.

## Kickoff meeting

The kickoff sets up a productive response by aligning all core people with critical activities. It is
**not** the initial planning meeting (an internal core-team meeting right after RFP release whose
outputs feed the kickoff).

- **Prepare materials first:** overall bid schedule, requirements checklist, proposal outline, draft
  executive summary, and content plans/worksheets pre-populated with draft themes, value propositions,
  and notes on where evidence should appear.
- **Everyone attends** — from the C-level champion to all teammates; this is where authority, roles,
  and responsibilities are established and shared.
- **Stick to a schedule** — keep it short (allow ~4 hours), instructive over inspirational, in two
  parts: overview of the effort, then response logistics.
- **Virtual kickoffs:** involve the core team ahead of time, distribute the schedule/kickoff package
  beforehand, focus on instructions rather than decisions, confirm understanding after each agenda
  item, and check progress more frequently.
- **Pitfalls:** confusing kickoff with the initial planning meeting; not allowing enough time to
  prepare (a rushed kickoff with missing docs sets the team up to fail).

## Daily team management

Day-to-day management is the engine of a successful proposal — you can rarely skip it for days and
catch up. Walk the line between injecting structure and letting the proposal run.

- **Tie daily activity to a written Proposal Management Plan;** the plan orients new joiners and lets
  the manager triage and set interim deadlines. Work that seems unrelated to the plan is a wake-up
  call.
- **Daily stand-up** — short enough to remain standing; same time each day; participants are the
  people accountable for sections/functions plus support. Report progress on near-term/long-lead
  items; **identify roadblocks in the stand-up but resolve them outside it** (unless trivial). Use a
  standard agenda; start/end on time.
- **Track the right things** — items with imminent deadlines or long lead times on the stand-up,
  everything else outside it; use a simple stoplight (red/yellow/green). Don't over-track to the point
  it steals writing time.
- **Listen for unspoken problems** — repeated identical status or silence from critical people
  warrants a one-on-one. Don't equate a statement of progress with actual progress.
- **Clarify roles** — distinguish **accountability** (getting something done, not necessarily doing it
  yourself) from **responsibility** (delegated to whoever can do it); watch for people unaware of their
  role's extent, or stepping into others' jobs uninvited.
- **Motivate** — managers act as supervisors: recognize extra effort publicly (per culture), provide
  tools/information, balance workload to avoid burnout, and respect personal/family obligations,
  especially on proposals longer than ~10 days.
- **Pitfall:** over-relying on virtual communication and losing the signal from facial expressions and
  body language.

## Reviews (color teams, content, proofreading)

Reviews are not check-the-box; good reviews often decide wins and losses. Review **early** — late
reviews leave no time to react; don't cut reviews to buy author time.

- **Color teams:** **Pink** (early, ~60% — structure, outline compliance, win-theme integration,
  content gaps, flow, graphics strategy); **Red** (near-final, ~90% — score each evaluation factor
  1–5 as a government evaluator would, find strengths/weaknesses/deficiencies, stress-test themes,
  compare to estimated competitors); **Gold** (final, ~99% — compliance with every shall, format/page
  count, cross-references, required forms, submission method/timing).
- **Content-development reviews** — interim check-ins on drafts during writing.
- **Proofreading** — a final, independent read (someone outside the bid team with strong writing
  skills) for consistency and accuracy in grammar/spelling/punctuation/style/formatting, and to catch
  serious mistakes (wrong customer/project name). Proofreading is **not editing** and spell/grammar
  checkers are **not** a substitute.
- **Run reviews well:** one **review-team leader** consolidates constructive feedback; keep a
  consistent core team, adding SMEs as needed; include executives and SMEs (their absence signals
  the price volume and technical solution won't get the scrutiny they need). Great reviewers can be
  trained; plan reviews deliberately.

## Production management

Production = formatting, publishing, and delivering the proposal. Though it happens at the end,
**plan it from the pre-RFP stage** — unreserved resources endanger on-time, compliant delivery.

- Identify people/tools/facilities; complete a draft production plan; get the schedule on the
  calendar; reserve equipment/facilities. Use production/editing/printing checklists scaled to the
  proposal. **Allow ~10%** of proposal time for production, scaling by size and delivery type; confirm
  team availability.
- **Re-read RFP instructions** (font, size, spacing, page counts) before production; update the plan.
- **Assign roles early**; note that smaller teams amalgamate roles and commercial work is often
  reactive with late strategy and a proposal manager owning everything through submission.
- **Consider outsourcing** production; source (and back up) vendors well before you need them and test
  them on an old proposal.
- **Plan delivery contingencies** — submit a **day early** (late delivery = no delivery); have backup
  delivery, IT support contacts, emergency/contingency plans, a backup proposal site, and frequent
  backups (two copies in different places; discourage individual backups that break version control).
- **Version control is critical** in production — an old section or out-of-date graphic can make the
  proposal non-compliant or commit you to the wrong scope/price.
- **No last-minute changes** unless not making them would cause non-compliance — and never without the
  direct, eyes-on involvement of the Proposal Manager and Opportunity Manager. Maintain senior-
  management visibility throughout.

## Managing internal risk

Internal risk is the risk of producing the offer itself to the required quality, on time, on budget.
Best practice: identify how internal gaps will be managed in the Proposal Management Plan.

- **Recognize** risk across **People** (enough of the right people at the right times?), **Resources &
  Tools** (technology/facilities to deliver?), and **Lessons Learned** (are prior lessons applied?).
- **Mitigate** — choose accept / mitigate / transfer / avoid for each risk; define alternative,
  backup, or parallel procedures; name an owner and clear decision points. Not addressing risk in the
  development phase inevitably hits cost and schedule; the Bid/Proposal Manager owns the acceptable
  risk level.

## Lessons learned

Gathered from your team and the customer after the award decision, to improve strategy and process on
wins and losses alike.

- Two debriefs: an **internal** lessons-learned debrief soon after submission (how efficiently you
  operated; strengths/weaknesses of your process) and a **customer** debrief after the award decision
  (how the customer evaluated bids; what really mattered; what you did well; where to improve). Do
  debriefs on **wins**, too.
- **Order:** conduct internal review → develop internal lessons → conduct customer-focused review →
  implement improvement plans. Hold reviews on all major engagements soon after submission, tailored
  to setting and maturity.
- **Run internal reviews** blame-free ("focus on the project, not the people"); capture feedback on a
  structured form; solicit input from the full BD team (capture/bid/proposal managers, coordinator,
  graphics/production, sales, solutions, knowledge, pricing/finance). Rate the effort on compliance,
  responsiveness, persuasiveness, and clarity, and on whether gates and reviews worked.
- **Customer-focused review** — whether you captured the customer's perspective and converted it into
  the proposal; begin it with the customer debriefing.
- **Structure** the sessions (send a pre-work document), reach consensus, document lessons, and give
  them to the bid-process owner; the sales/BD executive tracks recommendations into the org's
  improvement plans.
- **Pitfalls:** collected ≠ learned (track and apply); reviews are about process/decisions/resources,
  not blaming individuals; lessons learned start at opportunity identification and run the whole
  lifecycle; wins deserve analysis too.
