# Persuasion Toolkit

The four interlocking devices that turn capabilities into a reason to choose you: features/benefits/
discriminators, theme statements, value propositions, and proof points. They share one spine —
tie a customer benefit to something only you can credibly claim, and prove it.

## Features, benefits, discriminators

- **Feature = What.** Your capability.
- **Benefit = So what?** The solution to a customer problem that results from a feature. Customers
  buy benefits, not features.
- **Discriminator = Why us (and why it matters to you)?** What you offer better than any competitor,
  that the customer values.

Many proposals list features and leave the customer to connect them to benefits — don't. A deep
understanding of customer needs, competitor offerings, and your own capabilities lets you state the
benefit explicitly. Simple sales/opportunity process: meet the customer to determine needs → map
features/advantages to those needs → test solutions with the customer → confirmed advantages become
benefits you put in writing (e.g., feature/benefit tables) → compare against competitors to find
which are discriminators.

## The "So what?" test

After every benefit statement, ask "So what?" A reworded feature won't answer it; a real benefit
will. The test forces you to view the proposal from the customer's side. Technical SMEs and
incumbents are prone to thinking a feature is self-evidently a benefit (e.g., "93% reusable
components") — it becomes a benefit only when linked to a customer need. Where possible, have
someone non-technical but customer-minded judge feature vs. benefit statements. Make benefits
self-evident; don't let the customer draw their own conclusions.

## The discriminator sweet spot

Discriminators live at the intersection of **customer needs** and **your exclusive capabilities** —
something that matters to the customer and that no one else offers. Don't mistake mere features
(your brand/logo, large staff, many locations, defined processes) for discriminators; test each to
see whether it delivers a *unique benefit*. You can convert a shared feature into a discriminator by
attaching a unique benefit to it (e.g., past-performance learnings that let you deliver faster, at
less risk, or lower cost). Maintain the **customer's perception** as the reference point — bidders
routinely over-rate their own uniqueness.

## Theme statements

A theme statement tells the reader **why to select you** — linking the most important customer need
to a discriminating feature and a quantified benefit. Themes are not slogans; they are specific
statements woven throughout the proposal and reinforced in sections, paragraphs, headings, and
callout boxes.

**Formula:** *"[Benefit to the customer] because [discriminating feature], which results in
[quantified benefit]."*

Development:
- Flow themes from the executed capture strategy; build feature/benefit pairs mapped to the outline,
  starting at the top level (qualifications, technical approach, past performance). Each theme should
  identify a benefit linked to a feature, tie it to a customer issue, quantify where possible, and
  cite prior success.
- **Quantify, but stay believable and supportable.** A specific 33% reads truer than a round 30%;
  27% truer still. Support every number with evidence *soon after* in the proposal; if you can't,
  change the theme. Quantify collaboratively with the customer when you can.
- **Be concise** — one clear sentence (a "sound bite"). Cut every word you can, drop jargon, put the
  customer benefit first. Read it aloud: if you need a breath to finish, it's too long; if it sounds
  like something a normal person wouldn't say, simplify.

**Twofold litmus test:**
1. Could a competitor *believably* make the same claim? → must be **no**. (If yes, add detail that
   sets you apart.)
2. Could an evaluator cut-and-paste it into an evaluation form to justify your top rating? → must be
   **yes**.

Placement approaches (choose to fit the evaluation): one theme at the start of each section at a
uniform level; a theme wherever you discuss a significant discriminator; or a boxed series at the
start of major sections. Format all themes identically and place them consistently — evaluators come
to look for them. Focused, persuasive content matters more than the count or placement of themes.

## Value propositions

A value proposition establishes the value basis for the relationship: how your solution improves the
customer's business and how that improvement is measured. It offers something the customer wants and
a reason to choose you over competitors, and it forms the **foundation of the executive summary**.

- Sell **value based on client benefits**, ideally developed and tested with the customer. Go beyond
  theme statements by including estimated returns, timing of benefits and costs, payback period, and
  how results will be measured/tracked.
- Prepare a value proposition **per buyer/role** (a finance manager cares about cost; a marketing
  manager about market share), then a single **overarching summary** value proposition per
  opportunity, sensitive to each evaluator's needs.
- If colleagues resist being specific (fear of overpromising), use **proof points** — what another
  similar customer achieved with a similar solution — which remove the "guarantee" while keeping the
  persuasion (e.g., "customers in your sector reported an 18–23% NPS increase").
- Per Tom Sant, the average proposal decision takes ~6 minutes; value grabs attention and
  differentiates. Make the overarching value proposition the foundation of the executive summary so
  evaluators have a reason to pick you before they read the detail.

## Quantifying the payback (time / rate / value)

- **Time** — break-even/payback period ("pays for itself within six months…").
- **Rate** — a percentage / internal rate of return ("spend $100, expect 30% → $130 back").
- **Value** — money: net present value / economic value added ("saves $100k year one, $200k/yr
  thereafter; $500k over three years").

Make it **visual** (images speed understanding and add emotion) and focus on the **right kind of
impact** — the return the customer wants may not be financial. Where a benefit truly can't be
quantified, tie it to the buyer's mission/goals and use the buyer's own language.

## Proof points

Proof points are **verifiable facts** that give evidence for your features and benefits and support
win themes and discriminators. Without them, evaluators question whether features are proven and
benefits achievable, and they perceive performance risk.

- Types: project/performance data on similar contracts, case studies, success stories, customer
  quotes/testimonials, awards, third-party ratings — including a story of turning a problem or unhappy
  client into a good outcome (the *response* to a negative event can reflect better than a spotless
  record).
- **Start before the RFP.** Once you understand the win strategy, build a balanced store (not all
  testimonials). Mine prior solicitations from the same customer for recurring information needs, and
  gather that proof-point data early.
- **Run data calls** before and after RFP drop, internally and to teaming partners: name a POC,
  specify exactly what data you need, give clear due dates, and push for specifics ("many times" →
  "23 times"; "significant reduction" → "$43M"). A collaborative POC relationship makes the
  difference.
- **Keep a reusable repository**, updated periodically (some orgs appoint a Knowledge Manager who
  also feeds back, from debriefs, which proof points actually work).
- Make proof points **persuasive, tangible, and credible**: specific accurate numbers (not
  approximates), verifiable sources and references (get permission before quoting a customer),
  aggregated data to show size/strength, and interpretation ("explain what the numbers mean," don't
  just list them). **Weave them throughout** — technical narrative, action captions, resumes, past
  performance, even the pricing narrative — not only in callout boxes.

**Common failures:** waiting for the RFP; selling features with no proof; assuming stated benefits
are enough; generic claims ("high customer satisfaction" — how high?); burying proof only in boxes.
