# APMP Proposal Methodology

This skill encodes the APMP Body of Knowledge — the professional standard for competitive proposal
and capture management — as an operating doctrine. Other agents (Proposal Builder, Winning Strategy,
Price-to-Win, Compliance, Color Team, Past Performance, Document Scanner) produce artifacts; this
skill governs *how a winning proposal is built* and keeps everyone using the same definitions.

APMP is sector-neutral (federal, SLED, and commercial). Where a rule is regulation-specific (e.g.,
FAR certified cost-or-pricing data), it is flagged. Apply the doctrine; let the domain agents supply
the regulation.

## The prime directive: compliant AND responsive

Every proposal must pass two separate tests. Losing bidders pass one; winners pass both.

- **Compliant** = meets stated requirements and submittal instructions exactly — structure, page
  limits, formatting, every "shall/must/will," all forms. Non-compliance is the easiest reason for a
  customer to eliminate you (and, in government, invites a protest). Compliance keeps you *in*.
- **Responsive** = addresses the customer's stated *and unstated* goals, hot buttons, and values;
  proves you understand *why* each requirement matters; leads with benefits, priced to budget.
  Responsiveness gets you *ahead*. If two bids are both 100% compliant, the customer decides on
  responsiveness — or, absent it, on lowest price.

Rule of thumb given to writers: for each requirement, first state compliance ("We provide X"), then
add the responsive layer ("X matters because…, and ours does Y better, see proof point Z").

## How to apply this skill

1. **Locate the request in the lifecycle** (see `references/process-and-management.md`). Pre-RFP
   capture work and proposal work need different moves. Responsiveness and proof points are built
   *before* the RFP drops — if you're starting at RFP release, you're already behind.
2. **Pull the relevant framework below**, apply its decision rule or formula, and read the matching
   reference file only when you need the depth or the "why."
3. **Run the quality self-checks** before returning any proposal artifact.
4. **Feed the domain agents.** This skill decides the approach; the other agents execute it against
   the specific solicitation, NAICS, FAR clauses, and client data.

## Framework 1 — Requirements, compliance & the compliance matrix

Build a compliance matrix for **every** bid, regardless of size or timeline, **before** writing
begins. It is the master planning tool, the writer's guide, the reviewer's checklist, and — done
well — a selling tool.

- **Shred the RFP line by line.** Every "shall / will / must / required" becomes its own row. Build
  it in a spreadsheet so it re-sorts by RFP section or proposal section after amendments.
- **Parallel the RFP exactly.** Mirror the customer's numbering, naming, and action verbs. Fighting
  the customer's structure costs you evaluation points.
- **Hunt hidden requirements.** Many live in narrative, not in the instructions list. Finding them
  demonstrates understanding beyond mere compliance.
- **Grade compliance honestly.** Add a column for full / partial / non-comply and a *justification*
  for anything under 100% — customers need to know why a requirement can't be met.
- **Bid/no-bid trigger:** if the solution falls **below ~90% compliance** (or requirements change to
  push it there), revisit the bid decision. Flag disqualifiers — a non-compliant response there means
  the proposal isn't even evaluated.
- **Submit a response matrix** with the proposal so evaluators can locate each answer; if page
  limits forbid a separate matrix, fold its content into section headings.

## Framework 2 — Capture strategy & intelligence

Strategy is your pre-engagement position; tactics implement it. Opportunity/capture strategy =
win strategy; proposal strategy is a subset that carries the same message into writing.

Common terms — hold the team to these definitions:

- **Issues** = what keeps the customer awake at night (worries).
- **Motivators** = objectives they want (higher profit, lower cost, reduced risk, better quality/safety).
- **Hot buttons** = the ≤5 highest-priority issues+motivators for *this* opportunity. Record them in
  the customer's exact words; align the whole solution to them.

Identify the three buyer types and their distinct concerns:

- **Economic buyer** — final approval; cares about price/performance tradeoff and bottom line.
- **Users** — judge impact on their own jobs; concerns are emotional/subjective (reliability, ease,
  support, morale).
- **Technical buyers** — gatekeepers; can't say final yes but can say final no; score features
  against objective specs; often set the shortlist.

Tools: build a **Bidder Comparison Matrix (BCM) / SWOT** from the *customer's perception* of each
bidder, and update it repeatedly. Competitor weaknesses = your opportunities; their strengths =
your threats. **Ghost the competition** by raising the weakness of an alternative you rejected —
without naming competitors (independent, third-party evidence lets you go further).

Litmus test for a win strategy: *if the competition saw it, would it damage your chances?* If not,
it isn't a strategy yet.

## Framework 3 — The persuasion toolkit

**Features / Benefits / Discriminators**
- Feature = *What*. Benefit = *So what?* Discriminator = *Why us (and why it matters to you)?*
- Customers buy benefits, not features. Apply the **"So what?" test** to every feature statement —
  if the answer isn't a customer benefit, it's not done. Have someone non-technical but
  customer-minded check it.
- Find discriminators in the **sweet spot**: what the customer needs, that you offer, and no one
  else does. A shared feature becomes a discriminator when you attach a unique benefit to it.

**Theme statements** — say things (features do things).
- Formula: *"[Customer benefit] because [discriminating feature], which results in [quantified
  benefit]."* Reinforce throughout sections/headings/callouts, formatted identically.
- Twofold litmus test: (1) Could a competitor *believably* make the same claim? Answer must be
  **no**. (2) Could an evaluator cut-and-paste it into a scoring sheet to justify your top rating?
  Answer must be **yes**.
- Quantify, but stay believable: 27% reads truer than a round 30%; support every number with a
  proof point *soon after*. If you can't support it, change the theme.

**Value propositions** — the value basis for the deal; foundation of the executive summary.
- Quantify the payback three ways: **Time** (break-even/payback period), **Rate** (%/IRR), or
  **Value** ($/NPV/EVA). Make it visual; add emotion.
- Build a value proposition **per buyer type** (each has different hot buttons), then one overarching
  summary value proposition per opportunity. Develop/test with the customer where possible.
- If colleagues resist ("might overpromise"), use **proof points** (what a similar customer achieved
  with a similar solution) to remove the guarantee while keeping the persuasion.

**Proof points** — verifiable evidence for features/benefits; without them evaluators assume risk.
- Start collecting **before** the RFP drops; run **data calls** (named POC, specific data, due dates)
  internally and to teaming partners; keep a **reusable repository**, refreshed from debrief lessons.
- Make them persuasive, tangible (specific numbers, not "many"→"23 times"; not "significant"→
  "$43M"), and credible (cite sources; get permission before quoting a customer). Weave them
  throughout — not only in callout boxes.

## Framework 4 — Competitive Price To Win (cPTW)

cPTW is **not pricing.** It is an *externally focused, independent* assessment of the whole "Big S"
solution (technical, management, past performance, cost/price) competitors will offer — the target
position you must hit to win. Pricing is your *internal* effort to hit (or deliberately miss) it.

- **Price-to-Compete (top-down):** before requirements are known, use historical award/funding data
  to see where competitors bid and where the customer awards vs. budget. Feeds pursue/no-pursue.
- **Price-to-Win (bottom-up):** once requirements/evaluation are known, model each competitor's cost
  elements and cost-to-price strategy against a notional WBS, evaluate through the customer's
  criteria, and combine to find the optimum position.
- Keep it **independent** of the capture/proposal team to avoid "we're the best" bias. Present
  results as comfort-zone graphics, never single point values. Rely on ethically sourced,
  open-source intelligence and clearly stated assumptions. Iterate to award; capture lessons learned.

Depth: `references/competitive-price-to-win.md`.

## Framework 5 — Deliverable craft

Evaluators **scan**, they don't read. Engineer the document to be scored fast.

- **Organization:** plan content before writing; organize to the customer's structure; layer
  information (high-level → support → proof → address drawbacks → conclude). Consider **BLUF**
  (bottom line up front) for the whole proposal or each section.
- **Headings:** use the RFP's exact headings/numbering; make them **active** and benefit-led
  ("Reduced check-in to 7 minutes," not "About us"); verb headings for action, noun headings for
  direct answers; keep ≤3 levels, then run-in headings; keep them parallel and consistent.
- **Graphics:** write the **action caption first**, then Summarize → Explain → Visualize → Render.
  Graphics + words beat either alone; visuals raise persuasion (~43%) and recall. Every aesthetic
  choice needs a purpose; respect copyright; avoid visual noise; design for accessibility.
- **Page & document design:** lock a compliant template early (margins, fonts ≥10–12pt, columns,
  headers/footers, color); apply contrast, proximity, white space, consistency, balance, alignment;
  deliver as locked PDF. Never stray from customer-specified font/size/margins/page limits.
- **Cost & pricing data:** lead with a cost executive summary; show what comprises the price in
  numbers *and* graphics; distinguish price / value / added value
  (*perceived value ≥ budget ≥ price ≥ cost*). Government settings may require **certified
  cost-or-pricing data** and flow-down to subs — flag to the Compliance/Price-to-Win agents.
- **Past performance:** choose by **relevance, recency, quality of reference**; mirror SOW/PWS
  keywords (evaluations are often keyword-searched); design descriptions to be skimmed. Distinguish
  proposal risk (future approach) from performance risk (track record).
- **Resumes:** propose **named, available, more-qualified** people (no "will hire after award");
  a writer drafts them (not the SME); tailor to RFP requirements; standard compliant template;
  emphasize accomplishments over responsibilities; keep a corporate resume database.
- **Presentations:** audience first; content before slides; three-part structure; visuals not walls
  of text; benefit-led slide titles; rehearse.

## Framework 6 — Process, gates & team management

The generalized end-to-end BD process has **8 phases** gated by disciplined **bid/gate decisions**.
"The most successful organizations bid less and win more."

Every gate answers three questions: **Is it real? Can we win? Do we want to?** Gate types in order:
market entry → opportunity qualification → pursuit → bid/no-bid → bid validation → final review.
A losing bid is the costliest bid; a firm no-bid frees resources for winnable work.

- **Kickoff meeting** (not the internal planning meeting): held after core docs exist (schedule,
  requirements checklist, outline, draft exec summary, content plans). All contributors attend,
  including the C-level sponsor. ~4 hours, instructive over inspirational.
- **Daily stand-up:** short, same time daily; report progress on near-term/long-lead items; identify
  roadblocks in the stand-up but resolve them *outside* it. Distinguish accountability from
  responsibility.
- **Reviews:** run color teams — **Pink** (structure/compliance early), **Red** (score as the
  evaluator would, near-final), **Gold** (final polish). Add content-development reviews and a final
  independent **proofread** (not the same as editing). Review **early**; late reviews leave no time
  to fix. Great reviewers are trained and led by a review leader.
- **Production:** plan from the pre-RFP stage; reserve ~10% of schedule; version control is critical;
  submit a day early (late delivery = no delivery); no last-minute changes unless not making them
  would cause non-compliance.
- **Internal risk:** manage people/tools/lessons-learned risk in the Proposal Management Plan
  (accept / mitigate / transfer / avoid).
- **Lessons learned:** run an internal debrief soon after submission *and* a customer debrief after
  award — on wins as well as losses. Collected ≠ learned; track and apply them.

Depth: `references/process-and-management.md`.

## Framework 7 — Tools & systems

- **Proposal Management Plan (PMP):** evolves from the capture plan; roll out at kickoff; follow it
  but keep common sense. Choose plan type to fit the opportunity.
- **Scheduling:** schedule **backward** from the due date/time; reserve ~10–20% for planning/setup
  and ~10% held in reserve; minimize sequential and maximize parallel tasks; base estimates on
  metrics from past bids; change the schedule only for customer-driven events.
- **Opportunity/Capture plan:** action-oriented, opportunity-specific, developed *with* the customer;
  position the customer to prefer you before the RFP. An early draft executive summary is a key
  bridge from capture to proposal.
- **Content plans:** don't start writing until there's a shared picture of the finished product.
  Build a **robust topical outline** that is **MECE** (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive)
  and **uniquely assignable**; annotate it (themes, proof points, page budget, the question each
  section answers); use section content plans/storyboards or mock-ups for important sections. Apply
  "just enough" planning.
- **Knowledge management:** convert tacit → explicit; store reusable content as discrete objects
  under a clear taxonomy; KM is more change-management than IT.
- **SME interviews:** decide what to learn; ask open-ended questions; apply "So what?"; record;
  a writer (not the SME) drafts content, then the SME validates.
- **Library & style guide:** maintain peer-reviewed, up-to-date reusable content and a proposal
  writing style guide (clear, direct, jargon-free) aligned to the APMP BoK; always tailor reused
  content to the specific customer.

## Quick decision gates

- **Bid/no-bid:** below ~90% compliance, or many partial/non-comply rows → challenge the bid.
- **Pursue/no-pursue:** cPTW position doesn't meet business goals → walk away; conserve B&P funds.
- **Theme statement ships only if:** competitor can't believably claim it, AND an evaluator could
  paste it to justify a top score.
- **Benefit ships only if** it survives the "So what?" test with a customer-facing answer.
- **Value proposition ships only if** the payback is quantified (time/rate/value) and proof-backed.
- **Graphic ships only if** it has a summary/action caption written first and every aesthetic choice
  has a purpose.

## Quality self-checks (run before returning any artifact)

1. **Two tests:** is it both compliant (every shall answered, instructions/limits/format met) and
   responsive (hot buttons named, benefits before features, customer named first and more often)?
2. **Customer focus:** customer's words and numbering used, not paraphrased into your own terms.
3. **Evidence:** every claim/quantified benefit has a proof point nearby; no "generic" claims
   ("high customer satisfaction" — how high?).
4. **Discrimination:** themes and discriminators are things only *you* can credibly say.
5. **Scannability:** active headings, roadmap/section intros, graphics with action captions.
6. **Consistency:** headings, themes, and formatting are parallel and uniform throughout.
7. **Independence (pricing):** any cPTW output is external/independent, not a readout of your own
   solution.

## Anti-patterns to catch

- Compliance without responsiveness (or vice-versa) — both are required.
- Selling features instead of benefits; benefits with no proof points.
- Writing sections before themes are set and reviewed.
- Round, unsupported numbers; claims you can't verify.
- Deviating from the customer's structure/format without stating why.
- Treating Price-to-Win as "the lowest number from our own costing."
- Reviewing late; making last-minute changes; skipping the proofread.
- Building the compliance matrix after writing (blueprints after the house is built).
