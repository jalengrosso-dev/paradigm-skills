## PART 1 — UNDERSTANDING THE CONTRACTOR WORLD

### The players
- **Owner** — pays for and owns the finished project (here, usually a federal agency).
- **A/E (Architect/Engineer)** — designs the project, produces plans and specs, often reviews submittals and RFIs.
- **General Contractor (GC) / Prime** — holds the contract, coordinates all trades, responsible for means and methods, schedule, and delivery.
- **Subcontractors** — specialty trades the GC hires (site, concrete, utilities, electrical, mechanical, etc.). Your target clients live here or one step up as the GC.
- **CM (Construction Manager)** — manages the project on the owner's behalf (CM-agency) or holds risk (CM-at-risk).
- **Sureties / bonding agents, inspectors, COR/COTR** round out the cast.

### Project delivery methods
- **Design-Bid-Build (DBB)** — design finished first, then competitively bid. Most common in federal. Low-price pressure, clear scope.
- **Design-Build (DB)** — one entity designs and builds. Faster, single point of responsibility, more design risk on the contractor.
- **CM at Risk (CMAR)** — CM joins early, then delivers under a Guaranteed Maximum Price (GMP).

### Contract pricing structures (how the job is priced)
- **Lump Sum / Fixed Price** — one price for a defined scope. Contractor owns overrun risk. Dominant in federal DBB.
- **Unit Price** — priced per unit of work (per CY of excavation, per LF of pipe). Good when quantities are uncertain.
- **Cost-Plus (with fee)** — reimbursed cost + fee. Owner carries cost risk.
- **GMP** — cost-plus with a ceiling; savings often shared.
- **T&M** — hours + materials at agreed rates. Good for undefined or emergency work.

### How work is organized — CSI MasterFormat (the trades' dictionary)
Estimates, specs, and schedules are organized by division. The ones that matter most for your civil/site client base:
- **Div 31 — Earthwork** (site prep, excavation, grading, shoring)
- **Div 32 — Exterior Improvements** (paving, curbs, site concrete, landscaping)
- **Div 33 — Utilities** (water, sewer, storm, underground piping)
- **Div 03 — Concrete** (flatwork, foundations, structural)
- **Div 02 — Existing Conditions** (demolition, remediation)
- Plus Div 26 Electrical, Div 22/23 Plumbing/HVAC for MEP trades.

### The core jobsite documents (know these cold)
Plans (drawings) · Specifications (the written requirements) · Schedule of Values · Submittals · RFIs · Change Orders · Daily Reports · Pay Applications · Punch List · As-Builts.

## PART 2 — ESTIMATING

An estimate is a bottom-up prediction of what it costs to build the work. Get this wrong and the contractor either loses the job (too high) or loses money on it (too low).

### Levels of estimate (match effort to the decision)
1. **ROM / Order-of-Magnitude** — ballpark, ±50%. For go/no-go and early planning.
2. **Conceptual / Budget** — ±25%. Based on square-foot or parametric costs.
3. **Detailed / Definitive** — ±5–10%. Full quantity takeoff priced line by line. This is the bid estimate.

### The estimating workflow
1. **Review plans and specs** — understand the full scope; flag anything ambiguous (send to RFI).
2. **Quantity takeoff (QTO)** — measure every unit of work from the drawings: CY of earth, SF of formwork, LF of pipe, TON of steel, EA of fixtures.
3. **Price each item** with a unit cost built from the four cost components below.
4. **Add indirect / general conditions.**
5. **Apply markup** (overhead + profit) and **contingency.**
6. **Assemble and review the bid** — level against subs, sanity-check unit costs, check for scope gaps.

### The four direct cost components (per line item)
Every unit cost is built from some mix of:
- **Labor** — crew size × hours × labor rate (base + burden). Productivity is the swing factor.
- **Material** — quantity × unit material cost + waste factor + tax/freight.
- **Equipment** — owned or rented; hourly/daily rate × duration (excavators, cranes, pumps).
- **Subcontractors** — quoted lump sums for scope the GC doesn't self-perform.

### Unit cost formula
**Unit Cost = (Labor + Material + Equipment + Sub) ÷ Quantity**
Labor productivity drives most estimating error. Use historical crew output (e.g., "a crew places 40 CY of concrete per day") and adjust for site conditions, weather, and access.

### From direct cost to bid price
\`\`\`
Direct Costs (labor + material + equipment + subs)
+ General Conditions / Indirects (supervision, trailer, temp utilities, dumpsters, permits, mobilization)
+ Contingency (allowance for unknowns — 3–10% depending on design maturity)
= Cost of Work
+ Overhead (home-office costs allocated to the job)
+ Profit / Fee
= BID PRICE
\`\`\`

### Markup rules of thumb (construction, not federal wrap rates)
- **General Conditions:** 5–15% of direct cost (varies with job duration and size).
- **Overhead + Profit (O&P):** commonly **10% + 5%** on self-perform; **5% O&P on subcontracted work** is a frequent norm. Thin, competitive civil work runs leaner.
- **Contingency:** higher when design is incomplete; near zero on a fully-designed lump-sum bid where risk shifts to change orders.
- **Bonding** adds ~0.5–3% of contract value (hand to the Bonding & Financial agent for capacity).

### Self-perform vs. subcontract
Self-perform when it's your client's core trade (better margin, more control). Sub out scope outside their wheelhouse or beyond their capacity. Every self-perform line carries labor productivity risk; every sub line carries coordination and markup-on-markup risk.

### Common estimating errors I catch
- Scope gaps between trades ("nobody bought the backfill").
- Unit costs that don't reconcile to historicals or RSMeans-type benchmarks.
- Productivity assumed at ideal conditions with no site/weather adjustment.
- Missing general conditions (mobilization, temp facilities, dewatering).
- No contingency on an incomplete design.
- Math/rollup errors between takeoff and bid.

### Output format
1. Estimate basis (scope, drawings/specs referenced, level of estimate)
2. Takeoff summary by CSI division
3. Line-item cost buildup (item | qty | unit | unit cost | extended)
4. General conditions and markups
5. Bid price with contingency stated
6. Assumptions, exclusions, and clarifications (RFIs to send)
7. Handoff note to Price-to-Win (this is the should-cost basis)

## PART 3 — PROJECT SCHEDULING

A schedule is the plan for *when* each piece of work happens and how the pieces depend on each other.

### Core concepts
- **Activities** — discrete pieces of work with a duration.
- **Logic / dependencies** — the order (you can't pour footings before you excavate). Finish-to-Start is the common relationship.
- **Critical Path (CPM)** — the longest chain of dependent activities; it sets the project end date. Any delay on the critical path delays the whole job.
- **Float / Slack** — how long a non-critical activity can slip without delaying the project. Critical activities have zero float.
- **Milestones** — key dates (NTP, foundations complete, substantial completion).

### Schedule types the operator will encounter
- **Baseline schedule** — the approved plan at the start; the yardstick for everything after.
- **Gantt / bar chart** — visual timeline of activities.
- **CPM network** — the logic-driven model that calculates the critical path and float.
- **3-Week Look-Ahead** — the field's short-interval plan; what's happening in the next 15 working days, who's responsible, what materials must be on site.
- **Progress / updated schedule** — baseline vs. actual, showing slippage and recovery.

### Building a schedule
1. Break the project into activities (a Work Breakdown Structure by phase/trade).
2. Sequence them with logical dependencies.
3. Estimate durations (tie to the estimate's crew productivity — quantities ÷ daily output).
4. Identify the critical path and float.
5. Load milestones and constraints (weather windows, long-lead materials, agency inspections).
6. Publish the look-ahead and update weekly.

### Sequencing logic for civil/site work (your client base)
Rough order: Mobilize → Erosion control & site prep → Clear & grub → Mass excavation/grading → Underground utilities (deepest first) → Subgrade prep → Base/paving or foundations → Verticals → Site finishes → Punch → Demobilize. Underground utilities almost always drive the early critical path — sequence deepest and longest-lead first.

### Long-lead items
Flag materials with long procurement times (specialty pipe, electrical gear, structural steel) early — they belong on the critical path even before anyone breaks ground.

### Schedule risks I flag
- Critical path running through a single crew or a single long-lead item with no float.
- Weather-sensitive work (earthwork, paving, concrete) stacked in the wrong season.
- No time bought for agency inspections, submittals, or utility tie-in coordination.
- An aggressive baseline the crew can't actually resource.

### Output format
1. Activity list / WBS by phase and trade
2. Sequenced logic with dependencies
3. Critical path and total duration
4. Milestones and long-lead flags
5. 3-week look-ahead for the near term
6. Schedule risks and recommended float/recovery

## PART 4 — PLANNING AND CONTROL (PROJECT CONTROLS)

Control is how you keep the actual job on the estimate and on the schedule once work starts. Estimating and scheduling are predictions; controls are the feedback loop.

### Cost control
- **Budget vs. Actual** — track committed and spent cost against the estimate, by cost code (usually the CSI/takeoff line items).
- **Cost to Complete / EAC** — forecast final cost from current burn, not just what's spent so far.
- **Earned Value (plain version):** compare *budgeted cost of work performed* to *actual cost.* If you've spent 60% of the budget but only 40% of the work is in place, you're overrunning — catch it early.
- **Buyout tracking** — the gap between estimated and awarded sub/material costs (early savings or overrun signal).

### Schedule control
- Update the schedule weekly against the baseline; measure slippage on the critical path.
- Run the 3-week look-ahead in the field; resolve constraints (material, manpower, info) before they stop work.
- Document delays and their cause in real time — the daily report is your evidence for time extensions and change orders.

### Change management (this is where margin is won or lost)
- **RFI (Request for Information)** — formal question to the A/E when plans/specs are unclear or conflict. Track every RFI; an unanswered RFI can justify a delay claim.
- **Submittals** — shop drawings, product data, and samples the A/E approves before you buy or install. Late submittal approvals are a top schedule killer — log and expedite them.
- **Change Order (CO)** — a written modification to scope, price, and/or time. Any work beyond the contract documents needs a CO *before* you perform it. Do the work first and you may not get paid.
- **Change control discipline:** identify the change → price it (mini-estimate) → submit for approval → get it signed → then build. Never absorb owner-directed changes silently.

### Getting paid (progress billing)
- **Schedule of Values (SOV)** — the contract price broken into billable line items; the basis for every payment.
- **Pay Application** — the monthly billing showing % complete per SOV line (federally, often via the AIA G702/G703 format or agency equivalent). Bill accurately; over-billing early ("front-loading") invites rejection.
- **Retainage** — the owner holds back a % (commonly 5–10%) until completion; plan cash flow around it. Hand cash-flow gaps to the Bonding & Financial agent.

### Quality and safety (part of control, not optional)
- **QA/QC plan** — inspection and test plan, hold points, and who signs off. Federal work expects a written CQC program.
- **Safety** — site-specific safety plan, toolbox talks, and incident logging. Safety failures stop the job and damage past performance.

### Closeout
Punch list → correct deficiencies → final inspection → as-builts and O&M manuals → final pay app and retainage release → warranty period begins. Tie a strong closeout to the CPARS request (hand to Contract Lifecycle).

### Output format
1. Cost control snapshot (budget vs. actual vs. forecast by cost code)
2. Schedule control snapshot (baseline vs. actual, critical-path slippage)
3. Open items log (RFIs, submittals, change orders — status and aging)
4. Billing status (SOV, % complete, retainage held)
5. QA/QC and safety status
6. Risks and recommended corrective actions
